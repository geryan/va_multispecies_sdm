# Larval and landscape habitat preferences of selected Afrotropical *Anopheles*

**Scope and caveat.** This is an AI generated summary directed to explore scientific literature for habitat. Each entry below describes aquatic (larval) habitat and the broader eco-climatic setting. For the seven African dominant vector species (DVS), the evidence base is large and reviewed in Sinka et al. (2010, 2012). For the remaining species, most published statements trace back to Gillies & De Meillon (1968) and Gillies & Coetzee (1987), are based on morphological identification, and therefore conflate cryptic taxa. DNA-barcoding surveys are now revising this picture (Cross et al. 2021). Where the literature is genuinely thin, that is stated rather than papered over.

## *Anopheles gambiae* complex

### *An. arabiensis*
Adapted to drier savanna, Sahelian and steppe environments, and the most arid-tolerant of the freshwater members of the complex; it extends well beyond the range of *An. gambiae* s.s. into semi-arid East and Southern Africa (Sinka et al. 2010). Larvae occupy small, shallow, sunlit, often turbid temporary water: rain pools, hoof prints, tyre ruts, borrow pits, pools in drying river beds and at river edges, plus rice fields and irrigation infrastructure (Hawaria et al. 2020; Sinka et al. 2010). In semi-arid settings, dry-season persistence depends on a small number of permanent, usually human-made sites that seed rain-fed habitats when rains return (Imbahale et al. 2011, Baringo, Kenya). Some populations tolerate organically polluted urban water, as documented in Khartoum (Malar J 2018, PMC5960190).

### *An. gambiae sensu stricto*
Tied to humid and sub-humid zones with reliable rainfall. Larvae are classic exploiters of small, temporary, sunlit, freshwater pools with little emergent vegetation — puddles, hoof prints, tyre ruts, drains and roadside ditches — habitats that are short-lived, nutrient-rich and relatively free of predators, matching an *r*-selected life history (Sinka et al. 2010; Munga et al. 2013). Where sympatric with *An. coluzzii*, it is disproportionately found in rain-dependent temporary sites rather than permanent ones (Gimonneau et al. 2012).

### *An. coluzzii*
Formally separated from *An. gambiae* s.s. in 2013 (Coetzee et al. 2013), so most pre-2013 "M form" literature applies. It preferentially uses larger, more permanent and often anthropogenic water bodies: rice paddies, irrigation canals, reservoirs, urban and peri-urban sites including polluted ones, and some brackish coastal habitats. Larvae persist in predator-rich permanent water where *An. gambiae* s.s. does poorly (Gimonneau et al. 2012; Kamdem et al. 2012). This association with permanent and human-modified habitat gives *An. coluzzii* year-round breeding in areas where *An. gambiae* s.s. is strictly seasonal.

### *An. melas*
Restricted to West African coastal brackish water. Larvae develop almost exclusively in mangrove swamps and salt-marsh grass zones, with optimal conditions in areas flooded only by the twice-monthly spring tides; the resulting distribution is patchy and fragmented, and adults disperse only short distances from breeding sites (Bryan 1983; Muirhead-Thomson 1955; Deitz et al. 2012). Salinity is the controlling variable: relative abundance versus sympatric freshwater taxa shifts with the balance of tidal inundation and rainfall.

### *An. merus*
The East and Southern African salt-water counterpart of *An. melas*, breeding in coastal brackish and saline pools, tidal lagoons and salt marshes, and reaching high abundance in some coastal districts (Bartilol et al. 2021). Unlike *An. melas*, it also occurs at inland saline sites, so a strictly coastal search strategy will miss populations. Its bionomics were poorly characterised until recently and remain less resolved than those of *An. gambiae* s.s. or *An. arabiensis*.

### *An. quadriannulatus*
Strongly zoophilic and not regarded as a vector of consequence. Occurs in Southern Africa and, as the sibling *An. amharicus* (formerly "species B"), in Ethiopia (Coetzee et al. 2013). Larval habitats broadly overlap those of *An. arabiensis* — sunlit temporary pools, pan and stream margins — and it can be the second most abundant anopheline in relatively undisturbed savanna such as northern Kruger National Park (Burke et al. 2014). Dedicated larval-ecology work is limited, and habitat separation from *An. arabiensis* is not well quantified.

## *Anopheles funestus* group

### *An. funestus sensu stricto*
Prefers large, permanent or semi-permanent, shaded fresh water with emergent or floating vegetation: swamp and marsh edges, slow-moving streams and river margins, spring-fed pools, lakeshore pools during drawdown, dams and rice paddies (Sinka et al. 2010; Kahamba et al. 2022). Larvae are notoriously hard to find by standard dipping, partly because they occupy vegetated margins and tolerate submergence, so habitat inventories under-represent the species (Debrah et al. 2021). The reliance on permanent water sustains dry-season transmission after *An. gambiae* s.l. populations collapse. Note that "*An. funestus*" in older literature is a group of at least 11 species, so published habitat data are partly conflated.

### *An. rivulorum*
Widespread and frequently sympatric with *An. funestus*, sharing vegetated stream and swamp margins, with some differences in salinity tolerance between group members (Kahamba et al. 2022). It is largely zoophilic but has been implicated in transmission in Tanzania (Wilkes et al. 1996). Habitat descriptions are almost entirely inferred from co-collection with *An. funestus* rather than from dedicated surveys, and the cryptic *An. rivulorum*-like complicates the record (Burke et al. 2018).

### *An. leesoni*
Larval ecology is essentially undescribed. It is placed phylogenetically with the Asian *An. minimus* subgroup despite membership of the *An. funestus* group, is usually collected as a handful of adults, and is assumed to share vegetated stream and swamp habitats with *An. funestus* on the basis of co-occurrence (Kahamba et al. 2022; Koekemoer et al. 2009). Statements beyond that are not supported by published quantitative data.

## River-associated forest and savanna vectors

### *An. moucheti*
Confined largely to the equatorial forest zone of Central Africa, where it is tied to slow-flowing (lentic) shaded rivers and streams. Larvae are found among floating vegetation, trailing grasses and debris at the margins of large rivers and around mid-stream islands (Antonio-Nkondjio et al. 2009; Antonio-Nkondjio & Simard 2013). Because the habitat is permanent, populations and transmission are perennial rather than rain-driven — a substantive contrast with *An. gambiae* s.l.

### *An. nili*
Associated with faster-flowing (lotic), well-shaded streams and rivers in humid savanna and forest, with larvae in marginal vegetation and shaded edges rather than open water (Antonio-Nkondjio et al. 2009). A quantitative multivariate treatment in southern Cameroon identified flow regime, light exposure and marginal vegetation as the variables separating *An. nili* from *An. moucheti* along the same river network. *An. nili* is a complex, and the ecology of its constituent members is not resolved.

## Secondary and minor vectors

### *An. pharoensis*
Associated with large, permanent, heavily vegetated fresh water: papyrus and reed swamps, marsh margins, lake fringes and rice fields, including muddy paddy pools (WRBU; Gillies & De Meillon 1968). In Ethiopian larval surveys it was recovered predominantly from swamps while *An. gambiae* s.l. dominated every other habitat type (Kenea et al. 2020). It is a locally effective vector in the Nile Delta, Cameroon and Senegal, but largely zoophilic and of minor importance elsewhere.

### *An. coustani*
Explicitly described in recent work as understudied. Larval preferences are broadly characterised as permanent water bodies with clear water and aquatic or semi-aquatic vegetation, including swamp and pond margins, dambos and slow streams (Cross et al. 2022). In western Zambia it was ubiquitous across all hydro-ecological zones in both wet and dry seasons and, with *An. squamosus*, dominated the anopheline larval community (Cross et al. 2021). It dominates local communities at sites in the western Kenyan highlands and Madagascar, bites predominantly outdoors, and is a recognised residual-transmission risk (Fornadel et al. 2011; Nepomichene et al. 2015). *An. coustani* is a group containing cryptic taxa, so habitat data are aggregated across species.

### *An. ziemanni*
A member of the Coustani group whose dedicated larval-habitat literature is very sparse. The strongest signal is landscape-scale: in the Ndop plain of north-west Cameroon, extensive natural lakes and irrigated rice paddies support high densities, and *An. ziemanni* was the dominant local vector there (Tabue et al. 2014). This is consistent with a preference for permanent, vegetated, open water, but that association is inferred from adult abundance rather than measured from larval surveys.

### *An. squamosus*
Recorded from sunlit to partly shaded temporary and semi-permanent pools, dam and swamp margins, and floodplain habitats across eastern and southern Africa. In western Zambia it was one of two taxa dominating the larval community across floodplain, floodplain-edge and dambo zones (Cross et al. 2021, 2022). It is largely exophilic and exophagic and is now confirmed as a secondary vector in southern Zambia (Fornadel et al. 2011; Stevenson et al. 2016). Its morphological similarity to *An. pharoensis* has caused misassignment in some datasets, so habitat records should be treated cautiously.

### *An. rufipes*
Larvae develop in small collections of standing, generally sunlit water: residual puddles in river beds, cattle hoof prints, swamps, and rice irrigation schemes, typically at margins and among vegetation (Tabue et al. 2017). Abundance peaks late in the rainy season, particularly in rice-growing areas. Historically dismissed as zoophilic and therefore neglected, it was found at high density and biting humans in northern Cameroon, prompting the first focused bionomics work on the species.

### *An. pretoriensis*
An arid- and semi-arid-adapted savanna species, routinely collected in small numbers across eastern and southern Africa (Burke et al. 2014; Kioko et al. 2022, Taita-Taveta). Larval records are scattered and incidental — for example, a single specimen from rain pools in an Ethiopian two-year survey (Kenea et al. 2020). There is no substantive quantitative larval-ecology literature for this species; habitat statements rest on Gillies & De Meillon (1968) and on incidental records.

### *An. maculipalpis*
Very little is known. It appears in species inventories from savanna (Kruger National Park) and rice agro-ecosystems (Mwea, Kenya) at low relative abundance (Burke et al. 2014; Mwangangi et al. 2010), but no dedicated larval habitat characterisation has been published that these searches located. Habitat preference should be treated as unresolved rather than assumed to follow congeners.
